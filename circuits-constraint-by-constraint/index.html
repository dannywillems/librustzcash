24 - Circuits, constraint by constraint

Goal​

The Sapling Spend circuit, the Sapling Output circuit, and the Orchard Action circuit are the cryptographic heart of Zcash's shielded protocols. Earlier chapters described them at the "statement" level. This chapter walks each constraint clause, in the order the prover witnesses and the verifier checks, with an explicit "what attack does this clause prevent" for each.

The constraint counts are approximate; the exact numbers depend on gadget implementation and have shifted over time as optimisations landed.

0. The two constraint models​

R1CS (Sapling / Sprout / Groth16)​

A Rank-1 Constraint System: each constraint is

(∑iai⋅wi)⋅(∑ibi⋅wi)  =  (∑ici⋅wi),\Bigl(\sum_i a_i \cdot w_i\Bigr) \cdot \Bigl(\sum_i b_i \cdot w_i\Bigr) \;=\; \Bigl(\sum_i c_i \cdot w_i\Bigr),(i∑​ai​⋅wi​)⋅(i∑​bi​⋅wi​)=(i∑​ci​⋅wi​),

with wiw_iwi​ the wires (witness + 1) and ai,bi,cia_i, b_i, c_iai​,bi​,ci​ public coefficients. The Sapling Spend circuit has ∼100,000\sim 100{,}000∼100,000 such constraints. bellman is the toolkit.

PLONKish (Orchard / Halo 2)​

A table of cells with custom gates: each row iii has constraints of the form

G(w1(ωi),w2(ωi),…)⋅qsel(ωi)  =  0,G(w_1(\omega^i), w_2(\omega^i), \ldots) \cdot q_{\text{sel}}(\omega^i) \;=\; 0,G(w1​(ωi),w2​(ωi),…)⋅qsel​(ωi)=0,

where qselq_{\text{sel}}qsel​ is the selector polynomial. Plus permutation arguments for copy constraints and lookups for table membership. The Orchard Action circuit uses ∼211\sim 2^{11}∼211 rows.

The two models can express the same statements; only the arithmetisation differs.

1.2 - Clause: nk=[nsk] Gnk\mathsf{nk} = [\mathsf{nsk}]\,G^{\mathsf{nk}}nk=[nsk]Gnk​

Scalar mul of a fixed generator by a 252-bit secret. Using the fixed-base windowed comb gadget in sapling-crypto:

  • 252 bits of nsk\mathsf{nsk}nsk are decomposed.
  • For each 3-bit window, a constant-time table select picks one of 8 precomputed multiples.
  • The selected multiples are summed.

Constraint cost: ~750.

Attack on omission: if nk\mathsf{nk}nk is allowed to be any witnessed point unrelated to nsk\mathsf{nsk}nsk, the spender could inject a nk\mathsf{nk}nk they chose post-hoc, allowing them to predict nullifiers for notes they have not yet spent (or to forge nullifier collisions). The clause anchors nk\mathsf{nk}nk in nsk\mathsf{nsk}nsk.

1.3 - Clause: ivk=CRHivk(ak,nk)\mathsf{ivk} = \mathsf{CRH}^{\mathsf{ivk}}(\mathsf{ak}, \mathsf{nk})ivk=CRHivk(ak,nk)​

CRHivk\mathsf{CRH}^{\mathsf{ivk}}CRHivk is BLAKE2s-256 of the concatenated encodings of ak\mathsf{ak}ak and nk\mathsf{nk}nk (as their uuu-coordinates), plus parity bits. Implementing BLAKE2s in R1CS is expensive (one of the largest sub-circuits in Sapling, ~16{,}000 constraints).

The output is reduced modulo ℓJ\ell_JℓJ​ to produce ivk\mathsf{ivk}ivk. The reduction is a controlled bit-truncation rather than a full modular reduction; this is OK because ℓJ<2252\ell_J < 2^{252}ℓJ​<2252 and the top bits of ivk\mathsf{ivk}ivk are zeroed.

Constraint cost: ~16{,}000.

Attack on omission: an unmoored ivk\mathsf{ivk}ivk would let the prover claim ownership of arbitrary pkd\mathsf{pk}_dpkd​ they did not control.

1.4 - Clause: pkd=[ivk] gd\mathsf{pk}_d = [\mathsf{ivk}]\,g_dpkd​=[ivk]gd​​

Variable-base scalar mul: gdg_dgd​ is itself witnessed, so cannot use a fixed-base table. Implementation: 252-bit Edwards scalar mul gadget using strongly-unified addition.

Constraint cost: ~3000.

Attack on omission: a prover could spend a note addressed to an arbitrary (d,pkd)(d, \mathsf{pk}_d)(d,pkd​) they do not own.

1.5 - Clause: rk=ak+[α] Gak\mathsf{rk} = \mathsf{ak} + [\alpha]\,G^{\mathsf{ak}}rk=ak+[α]Gak​

Fixed-base scalar mul [α]Gak[\alpha]G^{\mathsf{ak}}[α]Gak (~750 constraints), plus one Edwards addition (~6 constraints), then equality with the public rk\mathsf{rk}rk.

Constraint cost: ~760.

Attack on omission: the publicised rk\mathsf{rk}rk would not be a re-randomisation of the actual ak\mathsf{ak}ak. An attacker could sign with their own key under their own rk\mathsf{rk}rk while spending a victim's note (since the binding to ak\mathsf{ak}ak would be lost).

1.6 - Clause: NoteCommitment​

cm=PedersenHashDnc(repr(v) ∥ repr(gd) ∥ repr(pkd))+[rcm]Rnc\mathsf{cm} = \mathsf{PedersenHash}_{D_{\text{nc}}}(\text{repr}(v) \,\|\, \text{repr}(g_d) \,\|\, \text{repr}(\mathsf{pk}_d)) + [\mathsf{rcm}] R_{\text{nc}}cm=PedersenHashDnc​​(repr(v)∥repr(gd​)∥repr(pkd​))+[rcm]Rnc​.

The Pedersen hash gadget (chapter 16) is the most heavily used sub-circuit. For an input of 64+256+256=57664 + 256 + 256 = 57664+256+256=576 bits:

  • ∼6\sim 6∼6 constraints per bit, so ∼3500\sim 3500∼3500 for the hash.
  • Plus the randomness term: 252-bit fixed-base scalar mul on RncR_{\text{nc}}Rnc​: ∼750\sim 750∼750.

Constraint cost: ~4250.

The result is the commitment; the prover does not get to choose it freely.

1.7 - Clause: Merkle path​

For each layer ℓ∈{0,1,…,31}\ell \in \{0, 1, \ldots, 31\}ℓ∈{0,1,…,31}:

  1. Witness the auth-path sibling at this layer and a boolean indicating "is the current node the left or right child".
  2. Compute MerkleHashℓ(left,right)\mathsf{MerkleHash}_\ell(\text{left}, \text{right})MerkleHashℓ​(left,right) where left/right are conditionally swapped based on the bit.
  3. Use the result as the current node at layer ℓ+1\ell+1ℓ+1.

The conditional swap costs ∼2\sim 2∼2 constraints; the Pedersen hash for a 512-bit input is ∼3000\sim 3000∼3000 constraints; the layer personalisation adds a small constant.

Per layer: ~3000 constraints. Times 32 layers: ~96{,}000 constraints. This is the dominant cost of the Spend circuit.

Attack on omission: a prover could spend an arbitrary cm\mathsf{cm}cm they invented, without it being in the tree. Money out of thin air.

1.8 - Clause: dummy spend handling​

If v=0v = 0v=0, the Merkle path check is skipped: a dummy spend does not correspond to a real note in the tree. The circuit implements this by computing the Merkle output and a "dummy override" output, then conditionally selecting between them based on v=0v = 0v=0.

The override does not set the anchor to a free choice; rather, the dummy clause makes the Merkle-path computation a no-op while all other clauses still hold. The effect: when v=0v = 0v=0, the cm\mathsf{cm}cm may be any well-formed commitment, but no anchor membership is claimed.

This is implemented as an "if-then-else" in bellman gadgets (boolean multiplexing).

Attack on omission: without the dummy mechanism, every spend revealed the bundle's true input count, leaking metadata.

1.9 - Clause: ρ=MixingPedersenHash(cm,pos)\rho = \mathsf{MixingPedersenHash}(\mathsf{cm}, \text{pos})ρ=MixingPedersenHash(cm,pos)​

ρ\rhoρ is computed in-circuit:

ρ  =  cm  +  [pos] Gρ.\rho \;=\; \mathsf{cm} \;+\; [\text{pos}]\,G_\rho.ρ=cm+[pos]Gρ​.

For pos\text{pos}pos bounded by 2322^{32}232, the scalar mul is cheap (~100 constraints). Plus one Edwards add (~6 constraints).

Total: ~110 constraints.

1.10 - Clause: nf=PRFnknfSapling(ρ)\mathsf{nf} = \mathsf{PRF}^{\mathsf{nfSapling}}_{\mathsf{nk}}(\rho)nf=PRFnknfSapling​(ρ)​

The PRF is BLAKE2s with key nk\mathsf{nk}nk and input ρ\rhoρ. Same BLAKE2s gadget as CRHivk\mathsf{CRH}^{\mathsf{ivk}}CRHivk.

Constraint cost: ~16{,}000.

The public output is nf\mathsf{nf}nf.

Attack on omission: the nullifier could be arbitrary, allowing double-spends.

1.11 - Clause: ValueCommitment​

cv=[v] V+[rcv] R\mathsf{cv} = [v]\,V + [\mathsf{rcv}]\,Rcv=[v]V+[rcv]R.

Fixed-base scalar mul on VVV with the 64-bit vvv (~250 constraints) plus fixed-base on RRR with 252-bit rcv\mathsf{rcv}rcv (~750).

Constraint cost: ~1000.

The result is checked against the public cv\mathsf{cv}cv.

Attack on omission: the published cv\mathsf{cv}cv could fail to commit to vvv, breaking the binding-signature equation and allowing value forgery.

1.12 - Clause: 64-bit range check on vvv​

The boolean decomposition of vvv is into exactly 64 bits, enforced by 64 boolean constraints during witnessing.

Attack on omission: a value >264> 2^{64}>264 would overflow the binding-signature value-balance accumulator, allowing implicit value forgery.

2.1 - Clause: subgroup check on gdg_dgd​​

The circuit asserts that the witnessed gdg_dgd​ is in the prime-order subgroup. For Jubjub, this requires checking that [ℓJ]gd=O[\ell_J] g_d = \mathcal{O}[ℓJ​]gd​=O, which is expensive but unavoidable.

In practice, the implementation uses an implicit subgroup check: the value gd=DiversifyHash(d)g_d = \mathsf{DiversifyHash}(d)gd​=DiversifyHash(d) is computed by the sender via cofactor-multiplication outside the circuit, but inside the circuit the prover only proves "gd≠Og_d \neq \mathcal{O}gd​=O" (one non-zero check) and the rest of the structure relies on the subgroup-membership being witnessed honestly.

Wait: this is delicate. If gdg_dgd​ is not in the subgroup, the recipient cannot decrypt (they would use [ivk]epk[\mathsf{ivk}] \mathsf{epk}[ivk]epk which would land outside the subgroup), so a malicious sender cannot benefit. But a witness-substitution attack might be possible in principle. The implementation defence: the canonical encoding of gdg_dgd​ in the encrypted note plaintext, combined with the recipient's re-derivation gd=DiversifyHash(d)g_d = \mathsf{DiversifyHash}(d)gd​=DiversifyHash(d) at decryption time, catches non-subgroup gdg_dgd​ from the recipient side. The circuit's explicit check is light.

This is the kind of subtlety chapter 13 warns about; reading the exact Sapling circuit code is essential.

2.2 - Clause: epk=[esk] gd\mathsf{epk} = [\mathsf{esk}]\,g_depk=[esk]gd​​

Variable-base scalar mul. ~3000 constraints.

Attack on omission: the published epk\mathsf{epk}epk could be unrelated to esk\mathsf{esk}esk, breaking note-encryption recovery for the sender (via ovk\mathsf{ovk}ovk).

2.3 - Why no anchor / no nullifier​

An Output creates value; it does not need to prove the new note is in the tree (it adds itself to the tree) and does not have a nullifier (it has not been spent). Hence the missing clauses.

3.1 - Halo 2 column layout​

The Orchard circuit uses ~10 advice columns over a 2112^{11}211-row domain. Each row is a small piece of computation; together the rows realise the full statement.

Custom gates groups (approximate):

  • q_ecc_add, q_ecc_double: Pallas point arithmetic.
  • q_sinsemilla: Sinsemilla chain steps.
  • q_poseidon: Poseidon hash steps.
  • q_lookup_range: range checks via lookup.
  • q_lookup_sinsemilla_S: Sinsemilla 10-bit chunk to point.
  • q_decomposition: bit-decomposition gates.
  • q_constraints: high-level "this equals that" gates.

Each is a polynomial identity over advice columns, gated by a selector.

3.2 - Sinsemilla in-circuit​

For the Note Commitment, the prover uses Sinsemilla:

  1. Bit-decompose the input (value, gdg_dgd​, pkd\mathsf{pk}_dpkd​, ρ\rhoρ, ψ\psiψ) into 10-bit chunks.
  2. Each chunk is looked up in the Sinsemilla generator table: (chunk,S(chunk))(\text{chunk}, S(\text{chunk}))(chunk,S(chunk)).
  3. Iteratively combine via the incomplete-addition gate.
  4. After all chunks are processed, add the rcm\mathsf{rcm}rcm blinding.

The Sinsemilla path costs ~300300300 rows for typical inputs.

Pitfall: incomplete addition fails when its operands coincide (chapter 13). The circuit must prove the operands are distinct, typically by witnessing intermediate accumulator values and asserting non-equality in a gate.

3.3 - Merkle path with Sinsemilla​

Each of the 32 Merkle layers uses one Sinsemilla hash of (layer index, left, right). The layer index is part of the personalisation DMH,ℓD_{\text{MH},\ell}DMH,ℓ​ which is encoded as a Sinsemilla domain.

Cost per layer: ~150 rows. Times 32: ~4{,}800 rows.

3.4 - The ρnew=nfold\rho^{\text{new}} = \mathsf{nf}^{\text{old}}ρnew=nfold trick​

The novel Orchard idea: after computing nfold\mathsf{nf}^{\text{old}}nfold, the circuit feeds it as ρnew\rho^{\text{new}}ρnew into the new note commitment. This eliminates the need for an extra Pedersen-hash based position-mix as in Sapling.

It also means that the new note's ρ\rhoρ is fully determined by the Action's inputs; the prover cannot freely choose it.

Implementation: an explicit copy constraint from "the row that outputs nfold\mathsf{nf}^{\text{old}}nfold" to "the row that takes ρnew\rho^{\text{new}}ρnew as input".

3.8 - Flag-conditional logic​

The bundle's flags determine whether spends and outputs are enabled per Action. If spends are disabled, the Merkle path check and nullifier publication are "no-op"d (specific dummy values substituted). If outputs are disabled, the new note commitment and epk\mathsf{epk}epk are dummy.

In-circuit: each clause is multiplied by a flag bit, and a "dummy substitution" gadget produces the public-input value when the flag is off.

This is more complex than Sapling's "dummy when v=0v = 0v=0" because Orchard allows mixed-mode Actions (e.g. an Action that only spends or only outputs).

5. Why each clause is necessary - consolidated​

The "attack on omission" notes throughout this chapter all reduce to one of:

  • Money forgery: prove a non-existent commitment as spent; prove a value larger than the input.
  • Double-spend: produce different nullifiers for the same note.
  • Identity theft: spend a note whose recipient was not the prover.
  • Metadata leak: distinguish dummy vs real spends.

Every clause in the circuits maps to one of these. If you cannot articulate which attack it prevents, the clause is suspect.

Public test vectors for the circuits:

7. Common circuit-author mistakes​

From audit findings and informal lore:

  • Underconstrained advice cells: an advice cell with no gate forcing its value can be set to anything by the prover. Verify every advice cell is used in at least one constraint with a selector on.
  • Off-by-one selector: a selector that is on at row iii but the gate references row i−1i - 1i−1 can subtly misalign.
  • Incomplete-addition coincidence: as noted, both inputs must be provably distinct.
  • Lookup-table collision: two distinct chunks mapping to the same point break the lookup soundness.
  • Public-input ordering: the prover and verifier must agree on which public input maps to which constraint. A swap is invisible in tests until a real attack exploits it.

9. The verifier's view​

The verifier of a Sapling Spend proof checks one Groth16 pairing equation. The "public inputs" presented to the verifier:

  • rk\mathsf{rk}rk (encoded as 2 field elements).
  • cv\mathsf{cv}cv (encoded as 2 field elements).
  • nf\mathsf{nf}nf (encoded as 1 field element by packing).
  • anchor\mathsf{anchor}anchor (1 field element).

The encoding is fixed and must match the circuit's expected order. If you change the public-input order, you must rerun the trusted setup.

For Orchard, the verifier runs Halo 2's verifier, which is more work (~10 ms vs ~7 ms) but uses no trusted setup.
